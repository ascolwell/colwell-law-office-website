I can usually tell within the first few minutes of a consultation whether someone's existing trust is actually doing anything, and it has nothing to do with how well the document is drafted. It comes down to one question: *"When you signed this, did anyone help you retitle your house, your accounts, or anything else into it?"*

A surprising number of people pause, and then say some version of "I don't think so." If that's you, I want to be direct about something before we go further: you probably don't have the plan you think you paid for. The trust document itself is just instructions. Until you actually move ownership of your things into it, those instructions have nothing to act on.

This is called "funding" a trust, and it's the single most common reason a perfectly well-written trust fails to do its job. Here's exactly what it means, and how to actually do it under Wisconsin law.

## What "Funding a Trust" Actually Means

When you sign a revocable living trust, you're creating a legal container — but an empty one. Wisconsin's Trust Code only requires that the trust have *some* identifiable property to be valid in the first place (*Wis. Stat. § 701.0402(1)(b)*), which is usually satisfied with a token amount at signing. Everything else you actually want the trust to control — your house, your bank accounts, your investments — has to be deliberately retitled out of your own name and into the trust's name afterward.

"Funding" just means that retitling process: changing the legal owner on each asset from "you, individually" to "you, as trustee of your trust." Nobody does this automatically. Not the bank, not the county, not the trust document itself. It's a series of separate, asset-by-asset steps — and it's the part of the process that's easiest to let slide after the signing appointment is over and the binder goes on a shelf.

## Why This Step Gets Skipped So Often

I've [written before about the most common estate planning mistakes I see](/blog/common-estate-planning-mistakes-wisconsin/), and an unfunded trust is consistently at the top of the list. A few reasons this happens:

- **People think signing is the finish line.** The signing appointment feels like the "official" moment, so it's natural to assume the work is done.
- **Retitling is tedious and spread across institutions.** Each bank, brokerage, and county register of deeds has its own process, its own paperwork, and sometimes its own confusion about what a trust even is.
- **Some attorneys hand over a binder and stop there.** Drafting the document and funding the trust are two different jobs, and not every attorney walks clients through the second one.

Whatever the reason, the result is the same: a trust that exists on paper but controls nothing.

## What Happens If You Never Fund It

If an asset is still titled in your individual name when you die, your trust's terms don't apply to it — because the trust never actually owned it. That asset passes under your will instead, and if it doesn't have a will covering it either, Wisconsin's intestacy statute decides who gets it.

Most trust-based estate plans include a **pour-over will** as a backstop — a short will whose only real job is to catch anything you forgot to retitle and direct it into your trust at death (*Wis. Stat. § 853.27*). That's a safety net, not a plan. Assets passed through a pour-over will still have to go through probate first, before they ever reach the trust. If avoiding probate was the reason you got a trust in the first place, an unfunded asset defeats that purpose completely — you've paid for the trust and still get the probate process you were trying to avoid.

## How to Fund Each Type of Asset in Wisconsin

There's no single form that funds everything at once. Each asset type has its own process:

### Real estate

Funding a house, cabin, or land means recording a new deed that transfers title from your individual name into your trust — for example, from "Jane Smith" to "Jane Smith, Trustee of the Jane Smith Revocable Living Trust." Wisconsin's conveyancing statute (*Wis. Stat. ch. 706*) sets out the formal requirements for a valid deed, and it has to be recorded with the register of deeds in the county where the property sits.

Two things people worry about here, and shouldn't:

- **Transfer fee.** Retitling your own home into your own revocable trust is generally exempt from Wisconsin's real estate transfer fee, since you're not selling anything or changing the real, beneficial owner.
- **Your mortgage.** If there's a mortgage on the property, federal law specifically protects this kind of transfer from triggering your lender's due-on-sale clause, as long as you remain a beneficiary of the trust (*12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(8)*). Lenders generally can't call the loan just because title moved into your own revocable trust.

I still recommend a courtesy call to your homeowner's insurance carrier so the policy reflects the trust as an additional insured — it's a five-minute call that avoids a coverage headache later.

### Bank and credit union accounts

This one trips people up because there's an easier-looking shortcut: naming the trust (or a person) as a payable-on-death (POD) beneficiary on the account instead of actually retitling it. Don't confuse the two. A POD designation only works *after* you die — it does nothing if you become incapacitated beforehand, which is one of the real benefits a funded trust is supposed to provide. To get that benefit, the account itself needs to be retitled into the trust's name, not just assigned a beneficiary.

### Investment and brokerage accounts

Same principle as bank accounts — the account itself gets retitled into the trust's name through your brokerage's internal paperwork.

### Business interests

If you own an LLC membership interest or closely-held stock, funding generally means executing an assignment of that interest to the trust and updating the company's records (the operating agreement, membership ledger, or stock ledger) to reflect the new owner. Check the entity's operating agreement or bylaws first — some require the consent of other members before an ownership interest can be transferred, even into your own trust.

### Retirement accounts and life insurance

These are the exception to the "retitle everything" rule. You generally do *not* retitle ownership of an IRA, 401(k), or life insurance policy into your trust — doing so would typically trigger immediate income tax consequences on a retirement account, since it's no longer owned by the original account holder. Instead, you update the account's **beneficiary designation form** to name the trust (or individual beneficiaries) directly.

Naming a trust as a retirement account beneficiary is an area worth a dedicated conversation rather than a DIY change — since the SECURE Act, how quickly a trust beneficiary has to withdraw inherited retirement funds depends heavily on how the trust is drafted. Get the beneficiary form right, but don't fill it out without talking to your attorney first if a trust is involved.

### Vehicles and tangible personal property

For most families, vehicles aren't worth the hassle of retitling — they typically depreciate quickly, are already covered by auto insurance regardless of registered owner, and Wisconsin's DMV process for adding a trust to a title can be more trouble than the asset is worth. The same goes for everyday personal belongings: furniture, jewelry, and other tangible items don't need to be retitled piece by piece. A single **assignment of personal property** — one document listing or generally describing these items as transferred to the trust — covers them as a group.

## The Shortcut That Makes Funding Easier: A Certificate of Trust

One real obstacle to funding is that banks and title companies sometimes ask to see your entire trust document before they'll retitle an account — which means handing a stranger a private document that lays out exactly who inherits what, and how much. Wisconsin law gives you a better option: a trustee can provide a short **certification of trust** instead, confirming that the trust exists, who the current trustee is, and what powers the trustee has, without turning over the full, private document (*Wis. Stat. § 701.1013*). If an institution you're working with insists on the complete trust instrument without even considering a certification, it's worth asking them directly whether a certification would satisfy their requirements — for most banks and title companies, it does.

## Funding Isn't a One-Time Task

Anything you acquire *after* your trust is signed — a new bank account, a refinanced home, a newly purchased property — needs to be titled in the trust's name going forward, or it sits outside the trust just like anything that was never funded in the first place. I recommend treating a review of your funding as part of the same periodic check-in where you'd otherwise review beneficiaries or update your plan after a major life change — a new account you forgot to retitle is just as much of a gap as one you never got to in the first place.

## If You Already Have a Trust and You're Not Sure It's Funded

If you're reading this and you're genuinely unsure whether your existing trust actually holds your assets, that's an easy thing to check — and an easy thing to fix if it doesn't. You don't need to start over or draft a new trust. In most cases, it's simply a matter of working through the asset list above and completing whatever retitling didn't happen the first time.

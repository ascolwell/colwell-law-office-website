Every few months, someone sits down with me and says some version of: "I know I should have a will, but nothing bad has happened yet, so I haven't gotten around to it." Then they ask the question that's actually on their mind: **what happens if I just... don't?**

Here's the direct answer: if you die without a will in Wisconsin, you don't get to decide who inherits your property. The state does. It's called **intestate succession**, and it's governed by Chapter 852 of the Wisconsin Statutes. The law doesn't know your family, your relationships, or your wishes — it just runs a formula.

## Who Actually Inherits Under Wisconsin Law

Wisconsin's intestacy formula works through a strict order of priority. It doesn't ask what you would have wanted — it moves down the list until it finds someone who qualifies.

### If you're married with no kids from another relationship

Your spouse gets everything.

> If there are no surviving issue of the decedent, or if the surviving issue are all issue of the surviving spouse and the decedent, the entire estate. *Wis. Stat. § 852.01(1)(a)1.*

### If you're married and have children from a prior relationship

This is where people are usually surprised. Your spouse does **not** automatically get everything — even if you've been married for decades. Instead, your spouse gets your marital property interest plus half of your remaining separate property, and your children (including the ones who aren't your spouse's children) split the rest.

> If there are surviving issue one or more of whom are not issue of the surviving spouse and the decedent, one-half of decedent's property other than [marital property and jointly held property]. *Wis. Stat. § 852.01(1)(a)2.*

This single provision is the reason blended families are one of the most common cases where I tell people a will is not optional — it's the only way to make sure a stepchild, a biological child, or a spouse ends up with what you actually intended, instead of what the formula produces.

### If you're not married

Everything goes to your children, divided per stirpes (meaning if one of your children has already passed away, that child's share passes down to *their* children).

> To the issue, per stirpes, the share of the estate not passing to the spouse, or the entire estate if there is no surviving spouse. *Wis. Stat. § 852.01(1)(b)*

### If you have no spouse and no children

The estate moves further down the family tree — to your parents, then to siblings (and the children of any deceased sibling), then to grandparents and their descendants, split between the maternal and paternal sides. *Wis. Stat. § 852.01(1)(c)–(f)*

### If no living relative can be found at all

In the rare case where the state genuinely cannot locate any qualifying heir, the estate escheats to the state of Wisconsin. This is uncommon, but it is the legal backstop.

## What This Formula Doesn't Account For

The statute is a formula, not a plan. It has no way to account for the things that actually matter to most families:

- **Unmarried partners get nothing.** If you're in a long-term relationship but never married and never registered a domestic partnership, your partner has no inheritance right under intestacy — no matter how many years you were together or how intertwined your finances were.
- **No guardian is named for minor children.** Intestacy governs *property*, not custody. If both parents are gone and no guardian is named in a will, a court decides who raises your children — and that decision can end up contested between relatives who each believe they're the right choice.
- **Stepchildren you never legally adopted get nothing.** If you helped raise a spouse's child from a prior relationship but never adopted them, they have no claim under Wisconsin intestacy, regardless of how you felt about them.
- **Friends, charities, and unmarried caregivers are invisible to the formula.** However close someone was to you, if they're not a spouse or a blood relative in the qualifying order, intestacy sends them nothing.
- **Everything still goes through probate.** Dying without a will doesn't skip probate — if anything, it often makes probate slower, because the court has to formally determine your heirs instead of simply validating a document that already named them. ## The Real Cost Isn't Legal — It's Personal

I want to be honest about something: the biggest cost of dying intestate usually isn't dollars. It's the family conflict that shows up when the formula's outcome doesn't match what everyone assumed would happen. I've seen adult children discover a stepparent is entitled to half the estate. I've seen unmarried partners of twenty years walk away with nothing while distant cousins inherit. These aren't hypothetical scenarios — they're the actual, predictable output of a statute that was never designed to know your family.

This is also the specific mistake I flagged in an earlier post on [common estate planning errors Wisconsin residents make](/blog/common-estate-planning-mistakes-wisconsin/) — not having a plan at all doesn't mean nothing happens. It means Chapter 852 happens, whether or not that matches what you would have chosen.

## How to Make Sure the Formula Never Applies to You

The fix here isn't complicated. A validly executed will lets you override every part of this formula — you decide who gets what, you name a guardian for minor children, and you can provide for a partner, a stepchild, a friend, or a charity that intestacy would otherwise ignore entirely. If you want the added benefits of avoiding probate altogether, a properly funded revocable living trust goes a step further. I've broken down [how to decide between a will and a trust](/blog/will-vs-trust-which-do-i-need-wisconsin/) if you're trying to figure out which fits your situation.

Either way, the requirements for a valid Wisconsin will aren't complicated, but they have to be followed exactly — I've laid those out statute-by-statute in [this earlier post](/blog/will-legal-requirements-wisconsin/).

## Bottom Line

Not having a will doesn't mean you avoid the decision about where your property goes. It just means a legislature made that decision for you, decades before they ever knew your name. For a lot of families, the intestacy formula happens to land close to what they would have chosen anyway. For blended families, unmarried couples, and anyone with a relationship the statute doesn't recognize, it usually doesn't — and by the time that mismatch surfaces, it's too late to fix it.
